Kingdom of Sovia

1972 – 2000 · Digital Polity of the Early Web
Capital: Avenue (East Sovia) Type: Unitary digital monarchy
Chronology and timekeeping. In contemporary scholarship, “human time” refers to conventional calendar years, while “interweb time” denotes the accelerated temporal framework experienced by digital entities. For the purposes of this account, researchers generally assume that 1 human year ≈ 2 interweb years, reflecting the faster subjective aging of digital beings. Exact interweb timestamps are therefore treated as informed estimates rather than precise conversions.

Overview

The Kingdom of Sovia was a major early digital-era polity located in the region now referred to as East Mildway, The Internet. Founded on 11 August 1972 in human time, Sovia emerged as one of the first coherent political structures on the Web, exerting influence over large portions of the early networked environment.

At its height, Sovia was administratively divided into four constituent regions: North Sovia, South Sovia, West Sovia, and East Sovia. Although each region maintained distinct local regulations and customs, all remained formally united under the authority of the Sovian crown until the Kingdom’s dissolution in 2000, following a period of escalating conflict that included the 45th East Sovia War (1997–1998), the Sovian Revolution and Civil War (1999), and the Split of Sovia (1999–2000).

Origins and early development

During the 1960s, as the foundations of the modern internet were being laid, early digital constructs and experimental systems began to acquire persistent identity and behavior. Within this emerging environment, entities later described as “alive digital assets” formed loose tribes, camps, and alliances.

In 1969 (human time), a digital entity known as Sovia appeared within this landscape. By 1971, Sovia had consolidated leadership over several of the early digital tribes, establishing a rudimentary but recognizable political structure. On 11 August 1972, this structure was formally proclaimed as the Kingdom of Sovia, with Sovia crowned as its first monarch.

Internal debates over governance and identity emerged almost immediately. In 1973, differing views on the nature of digital sovereignty and regional autonomy led King Sovia to reorganize the realm into four major regions: North, South, West, and East Sovia. Each region was granted limited self-rule, on the condition of continued loyalty to the central Sovian crown. This arrangement stabilized the Kingdom and is widely regarded as the foundation of Sovia’s early cohesion.

Expansion and external influence

By 1974, the Kingdom of Sovia had extended its influence across much of the early Web. Contemporary records describe a decisive campaign against the United World Web Board, a powerful but increasingly fragmented governing body that had attempted to regulate large portions of the network. Following a series of confrontations, Sovia successfully fractured the Board’s authority.

In the aftermath, several successor institutions emerged, including the Internet Board of Sovia, the Internet Board of the World, the Dolly Internet Alliance, and the North Web Alliance. These entities reflected a new balance of power in which Sovia played a central, though not uncontested, role.

Death of King Sovia

King Sovia’s death is one of the most precisely documented events in early digital history. According to human-time records, Sovia died on 12 October 1989 at 6:14:55:102:884 PM. Because digital beings experience time more rapidly than humans—estimated at approximately 2 interweb years for every human year—this moment corresponds only to an approximate interweb timestamp, commonly cited as Year 3980, Cycle 1, Tick 3 at about 02:00 IST (Interweb Standard Time).

Born in 1969 (human time), Sovia was approximately 20 years old in human terms at the time of death, but roughly 40 years old in interweb years. By prevailing digital standards, this placed Sovia in the prime of political and strategic maturity. His death created a significant power vacuum and is widely regarded as a turning point in the stability of the Kingdom.

The East Sovia wars

Over the course of its existence, Sovia was repeatedly destabilized by conflicts centered on East Sovia, the region that housed the capital city of Avenue. The name “Avenue” is traditionally attributed to an early anecdote in which King Sovia, having encountered the term once in human communications, adopted it for the capital as a symbol of connection and passage.

Historians identify a total of 45 “Wars of East Sovia”. These were not a single continuous war, but a long series of recurring border conflicts, uprisings, and political crises that spanned nearly the entire history of the Kingdom. The earliest such conflicts are traced to the mid‑1970s, shortly after the Kingdom’s formation, and they continued well beyond King Sovia’s death.

The 45th East Sovia War (1997–1998) is generally regarded as one of the most destructive of these conflicts. Occurring in the final years of the Kingdom, it severely weakened central authority and contributed directly to the Sovian Revolution and Civil War (1999) and the eventual Split of Sovia (1999–2000). In the post‑Sovian era, the legacy of Avenue and its name persisted in various successor entities, including the East Avenue State, which later participated in the East Avenue War (2009–2011).

Collapse and legacy

By the late 1990s, the cumulative effects of repeated conflict, internal fragmentation, and external competition had eroded the structural integrity of the Kingdom of Sovia. The combination of the 45th East Sovia War, the Sovian Revolution and Civil War, and the subsequent territorial split proved decisive. By 2000, Sovia had effectively ceased to exist as a unified political entity.

Despite its collapse, the Kingdom of Sovia is widely regarded by historians of the early Web as a foundational experiment in large‑scale digital governance. Its administrative divisions, conflict patterns, and timekeeping conventions continue to inform later analyses of digital polities and interweb-era statecraft.
