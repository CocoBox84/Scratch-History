Start of the Republic (1999–2002)

Coat Of Arms of the Scratch Republic.
"Από τη σκόνη και τη στάχτη, ανεβαίνει και μεγαλώνει."
means "From dust and ashes, it rises and grows." in Greek
Flag of the Scratch Republic.
Similar to the flag of the "Kingdom of Sovia", but includes an 'S' in the middle.

After 45th East Sovia war (of the internet) (1997-1998), the Internet Board of Sovia, Internet board of the world, and the Dolly Internet Alliance declared that the refugees of the "Turtlepaint Project" (later the "Scratch Project") would become its own nation under the seal of 'The Internet'. It officially became the "Turtlepaint Republic" on 4/7/1999. On 8/3/2002, the "Turtlepaint Republic" was changed to the "Scratch Republic" after the MIT military conquered the area. It was not simply a name change; it changed the Republic's government completely after the restart of the "TurtlePaint project" to MIT's "Scratch Project".

Body's of the republic

The republic itself is the top head of anything that has to do with Scratch. It controls the inner government, as well as the outer government. The inner is replaceable, and can be switched and swapped; it also has no limits other than the rules of the republic itself. The first inner government was the "Scratch Republic of West Sovia" (2002 - 2003). When an inner government collapses, it can be replaced with a better one, preventing the risk of the collapse of the full republic.

Scratch Republic of West Sovia (2002–2003)

Flag of the Scratch Republic of West Sovia.

The "Scratch Republic of West Sovia" was the first nation to take the role of "Inner government of the Scratch Republic". When Sovia collapsed and split into four in 2000, West Sovia promised the "Scratch Republic" protection from its enemies if it refused to rebel. So the "Scratch Republic of West Sovia" was born. But in 2003, after the collapse of West Sovia, the Scratch Republic of West Sovia did too. And the "Scratch Empire" took its spot. In 2002, development of Scratch would start.

Why West Sovia Became the First Inner Government

West Sovia was the most stable of the four Sovian successor states. Unlike East Sovia or North Sovia, which were plagued by malware uprisings and data corruption, West Sovia maintained a functioning digital infrastructure. This made it an ideal partner for the young Scratch Republic, which lacked the resources to defend itself.

The alliance was formalized in late 2002, creating the Scratch Republic of West Sovia. West Sovia provided military protection, server space, and early moderation forces. In return, the Scratch Republic offered technological support and access to the developing Scratch platform.

The Collapse of West Sovia

By 2003, West Sovia began to fracture under internal disputes. Rival factions argued over control of the Sovian archives, and a series of cyber‑attacks destabilized the region. When West Sovia finally collapsed, its protective shield over the Scratch Republic vanished.

Without its guardian state, the Scratch Republic was forced to reorganize quickly. This power vacuum led directly to the rise of the Scratch Empire.

Scratch Empire (2003-2004)

The new inner government, the "Scratch Empire", did not rely on foreign powers. But it was weak from the start. It was also temporary, collapsing after President Jack Houlms overthrew it and built the "State of Scratch", which still stands today.

Formation of the Empire

After the fall of West Sovia, the Republic feared another collapse. In response, a faction of leaders proposed a more centralized system: the Scratch Empire. Its goal was to unify all Scratch‑related territories under a single, strong authority that could not be easily overthrown by outside forces.

The Empire introduced strict organizational structures, including the Imperial Block Registry, which attempted to standardize all scripts and sprites. While efficient on paper, these policies were deeply unpopular among creators who valued freedom and experimentation.

Internal Weaknesses

The Empire struggled from the start. Its leadership was divided between traditionalists who wanted to preserve Turtlepaint‑era rules and reformists who pushed for rapid modernization. These internal conflicts slowed development and frustrated the community.

The Overthrow

By 2004, the Empire had lost nearly all support. When Jack Houlms returned to power, he peacefully dismantled the Empire and replaced it with the State of Scratch, a more flexible and democratic system that still exists today.

State of Scratch

The "State of Scratch" began after the collapse and overtaking of the Scratch Empire by Jack Houlms. He united the "Scratch Project" and the Republic into one. Creating the State of Scratch in 2004. It is currently still the head government in the Republic.

Early Structure of the Republic

In its earliest years, the Scratch Republic operated more like a loose federation than a unified nation. The refugees of the Turtlepaint Project brought different customs, coding styles, and political ideas. To keep order, the Republic established the First Assembly of Blocks, a council responsible for drafting the earliest laws of Scratch. These laws included the first guidelines for sprites, early project‑sharing rules, and the foundation of what would later become the Scratch Community Guidelines.

During this period, the Republic adopted the green flag as its symbol of unity. It represented the idea that every project — no matter how small — begins with a single click. The motto “From dust and ashes, it rises and grows” became a reminder of the Republic’s humble origins.

Early Conflicts

The Republic faced constant pressure from neighboring digital states left over from the collapse of Sovia. Many Sovian factions believed the Scratch refugees had taken land that once belonged to them. These tensions would eventually lead to the Republic seeking protection from West Sovia in 2002.

    Kevin Qubes

    Known for his creative ideas and ambitious plans, Kevin Qubes quickly rose to prominence as the Republican nominee. Although he lost to Jack in the first election, Kevin remained popular and continued to challenge Jack’s leadership in later years.

    (Each presidential term lasted three years.)

  • The First Debate (May 2002)

    The debate determined who would lead the Scratch project during its early development. The winner would also become the very first president of the project.

    Kevin Qubes and Jack Houlms during the 2002 debate

  • Jack Wins the Election (2002)

    On election day, Jack Houlms won by a margin of just 10 votes.

Houlms Presidential Term (2003–2005)

  • 2003: The First Prototype

    Under Jack’s leadership, the first prototype of Scratch (sometimes called Scratch 0.1) was created in October 2003. This early version introduced basic block‑based coding, inspired by Logo Blocks, and set the foundation for Scratch’s drag‑and‑drop style.

    Jack proudly declared it a “victory for cats everywhere.”

  • 2004: Major Redesign

    In 2004, the interface was redesigned. The stage moved to the right side of the screen, while the coding blocks shifted to the left — similar to how Scratch looks today.

    New features like broadcast messages and named scripts were tested, giving creators more power to control their projects.

    Jack called this “the golden age of block politics.”

  • Also 2004: Scratch Empire collapses, replaced by "State of Scratch"

    The Scratch Empire collapses, uniting the job of the "President of the Scratch project" and the "Head of the inner government of the Scratch Republic" into one. Just the "President of the State of Scratch" or "President of the Scratch Republic", although he is under the republic, the new system works so well that that is what he is sometime called ... Add here ...

  • 2005: Towards Scratch 1.0

    By 2005, Scratch was starting to resemble the version that would later be released publicly. Developers experimented with sprite creation tools, including a purple star button that allowed users to add new sprites instantly.

    Meanwhile, Kevin Qubes criticized Jack’s leadership, claiming the project needed “more cubes and fewer cats.” This set the stage for the next election.

The Second Election (2005)

  • Rising Opposition

    By late 2005, Kevin Qubes had regained momentum. He organized rallies and protests, arguing that Jack Houlms had failed to deliver on his promises of “block stability.” His slogan became: “More cubes, fewer cats!”

  • Scratch B. A. Cat Enters the Race

    Scratch Bitmap Alexander Cat — better known as Scratch Cat — stepped into the spotlight. As the new mascot and default sprite of the editor, he argued that the roles of mascot and president should be united. Critics, especially Qubes, accused him of “wanting all the power.”

  • The Campaign Trail

    The 2005 campaign was fierce. Jack Houlms, weakened by criticism, chose not to run again. This left Qubes and Scratch Cat to battle for leadership. Debates focused on whether the project should prioritize innovation (Scratch Cat’s platform) or structure (Qubes’ platform).

  • Election Results

    In a dramatic finish, Scratch B. A. Cat narrowly defeated Kevin Qubes. His victory speech promised “a brighter, scratchier future.”

    This marked the beginning of a new era, leading directly into the development of Scratch 1.0.

Qubes Presidential Term (2005–2007)

  • 2005: Interim Leadership

    After Scratch Cat’s accident, Kevin Qubes stepped in as acting president. Although he was not allowed to officially launch Scratch, he oversaw the creation of the first beta website, where early testers could share projects in a limited way.

  • 2006: The Beta Years

    Throughout 2006, Qubes managed the beta community. He introduced stricter “cube‑based” policies, requiring every project to include at least one square sprite.

    While controversial, this period also saw the rise of Scratch’s first online community, with kids around the world experimenting with animations and games.

    Meanwhile, Scratch Cat slowly recovered from his injuries, preparing for a comeback.

  • 2007: The Launch of Scratch 1.0

    By early 2007, Scratch was ready for its official release. Qubes reluctantly handed the spotlight back to Scratch Cat, who returned just in time to preside over the launch of Scratch 1.0 in May 2007.

    This marked the end of Qubes’ unusual presidency and the beginning of Scratch’s public era, where millions of young creators would soon join the movement.

The Third Election (2007)

  • Scratch Cat Returns

    After recovering from his accident, Scratch B. A. Cat announced his return to politics. He promised to finally unite the roles of mascot and president, declaring: “No more cubes versus cats — only Scratchers together!”

  • Kevin Qubes’ Last Stand

    Kevin Qubes, still popular among his supporters, ran once again. His platform focused on “orderly projects” and stricter rules for sprites. However, many felt his cube‑focused policies had stifled creativity during his interim presidency.

  • The 2007 Debate

    The debate centered on the future of Scratch as it prepared for its first public release. Scratch Cat argued for openness and creativity, while Qubes emphasized structure and discipline.

    Scratch Cat and Kevin Qubes face off in the 2007 debate

  • Election Results

    In May 2007, Scratch Cat won a decisive victory, just in time to preside over the official launch of Scratch 1.0. His victory speech proclaimed: “From this day forward, every kid can code!”

  • A New Era Begins

    With Scratch Cat as both mascot and president, the project entered its public era. Millions of young creators soon joined the community, marking the start of Scratch’s global legacy.

Scratch Bitmap Alexander Cat’s Long Term (2007–2012)

(Extended due to emergencies, the East Avenue War, the decline of Java Applets, and the development of Scratch 2.0.)

  • 2008: Stabilizing the Community

    With Scratch 1.0 launched, Scratch Cat focused on stabilizing the growing community. He introduced the first official forums, where Scratchers debated ideas — and occasionally argued about whether cats or cubes were superior.

    The Java Player became available for running projects online, though it was slow and buggy. Most users preferred downloading projects, editing them, and re‑sharing — the earliest form of remixing.

  • 2009: The Expansion Era

    Scratch Cat oversaw the expansion of the Scratch website. New categories like “Animations” and “Games” were added, and the first wave of Scratch memes appeared. Qubes criticized this as “chaotic creativity,” but the people loved it.

  • 2009: The Flash Player Revolution

    A developer began experimenting with Adobe Flex (AS3) and created a new Flash‑based project player. It was far faster than the old Java Player, which was being phased out. This Flash Player became the foundation for Scratch 2.0.

  • 2010: The Great Server Crash

    A massive server outage nearly brought the Scratch Republic to its knees. Scratch Cat declared a “state of emergency” and rallied the community to rebuild. This event became known as the “Great Lag Crisis of 2010.”

  • 2011: Preparing for the Future

    Behind the scenes, developers began working on Scratch 2.0. Scratch Cat promised that the new version would bring “bigger blocks, brighter colors, and fewer crashes.” Meanwhile, Kevin Qubes quietly plotted another comeback.

  • 2012: The Road to Scratch 2.0

    By 2012, anticipation for Scratch 2.0 was at an all‑time high. Scratch Cat’s long presidency was extended to ensure stability during the transition. Rumors of a new election began to circulate, setting the stage for the next great political showdown.

The Big Election (Late 2012)

  • Context

    This election was destined to be historic. Scratch was undergoing its biggest change ever with the move to Scratch 2.0. Kevin Qubes chose not to run again, and Scratch Cat (Bitmap) was barred from running due to term limits. With no former presidents in the race, new candidates emerged.

  • Scratch V. W. Cat (Republican)

    Scratch Vector Walker Cat — better known as Vector — was unrelated to Bitmap Scratch Cat, but both were known simply as “Scratch Cat.” Vector was a sleek HD vector graphic, perfectly suited for Scratch 2.0’s new vector‑based editor.

    To avoid confusion, the old mascot became known as “Bitmap,” while the new one was “Vector.”

  • Alpha S. V. Cat (Democrat)

    Vector’s older brother, Alpha Scratch Vector Cat, also entered the race. Alpha had occasionally appeared as a stand‑in for Bitmap in older parts of the Scratch website, and even on the 404 error page. Supporters praised him as a “stunt double turned leader.”

    He was also a strong advocate for the new Flash Player, making the election highly competitive.

  • The First Debate (November 2012)

    The debate determined who would lead Scratch into the 2.0 era. The two brothers clashed fiercely over the future of the platform.

    Vector and Alpha Cat face off in the 2012 debate

Transition of Power

  • Bitmap Steps Down, Vector Steps In

    Bitmap Scratch Cat, after five years in office, officially stepped down. He congratulated Vector and reminded the community that “whether bitmap or vector, Scratch belongs to everyone.”

    Alpha conceded gracefully, though his supporters vowed to keep pushing for more “retro bitmap values.” Despite losing, Alpha never truly disappeared. He continued to make cameo appearances across the site — showing up on error pages like 404 and 403, and even appearing on the new “Scratch Project cannot display. Flash Player is missing or disabled” screen. These ghostly appearances kept Alpha in the public eye, fueling conspiracy theories that he was still running Scratch from the shadows.

(Summary of) Vector’s Presidential Term (2013–2016)

  • 2013: The Launch of Scratch 2.0

    Vector presided over the release of Scratch 2.0 on May 9, 2013, introducing the online editor, cloud variables, and a redesigned interface. His sleek vector look symbolized the new era.

  • 2014: The Golden Vector Age

    Scratch’s popularity exploded worldwide. Vector was praised for modernizing the platform, though whispers began about his over‑reliance on Adobe Flash Player, which powered Scratch 2.0.

  • 2015: The Flash Backlash

    As Flash Player’s security flaws grew, protests erupted. Bitmap led the Anti‑Flash March, and Vector’s stubborn defense of Flash began to tarnish his reputation.

  • October 26, 2015: Homepage Redesign

    On October 26, 2015, the Scratch homepage was redesigned into the style that still resembles today’s layout. Felix helped design it, and Vector approved the changes. This was before the protests began.

    Later, Felix would help redesign more pages: the messages page in 2017, the explore page in 2018, and the darker blue header that became known as the “Scratch 3.0 style.” Although most of these redesigns happened during Scratch 2.0, they were nicknamed “Scratch 3.0 style pages” because they anticipated the next era.

  • 2016: Decline and Defeat

    By 2016, Vector’s presidency was collapsing under the weight of Flash controversies and Bitmap’s sudden death. Felix Whiskerton rose as the voice of change, defeating Vector in the election and ending his term.

  • Felix’s First Appearance to the Scratch Community (April Fools 2016)

    On April Fools’ Day 2016, Felix unveiled Scratch Cat’s new design — himself — to the community. Vector remained the default sprite in the editor because Felix’s design had “too many moving parts” for beginners, but Felix became the official mascot. The April Fools joke was called “Where in the World is Scratch Cat?”, where Scratch Cat went on vacation and users created projects guessing his location.

    The first projects shared by the official Scratch Cat account

  • Scratch’s Big Day (June 2016)

    In June 2016, Scratch collaborated with the Hour of Code. Cartoon Network joined in, creating a Scratch account and featuring the Powerpuff Girls on the homepage. Google also promoted Blockly, a block‑based language often compared to Scratch. This collaboration helped inspire Scratch 3.0, which would later be built on Blockly’s framework.

    Scratch Cat flying with the Powerpuff Girls (and a flying taco) on the Scratch homepage, June 2016

Whiskerton Announces Scratch 3.0 (2017)

  • At the Scratch Day 2017 conference, President Felix Whiskerton gave a historic speech. This was the moment Scratch 3.0 was officially announced to the world:

    "... Yes, as you may, or may not have heard. Scratch 3.0 is official. It will be the future of Scratch — faster, safer, and more creative than ever before. Together, we will leave Flash behind and build something new."

    The announcement electrified the community. Scratchers began speculating about new blocks, mobile compatibility, and the long‑awaited HTML5 editor. The phrase "Scratch 3.0 is official" became a rallying cry across the forums.

President Kevin Qubes Dies at 75

  • On October 27, 2017, Kevin Qubes — former president and long‑time rival of Jack Houlms — passed away at the age of 75 from a heart attack linked to liver complications. His death shocked the Scratch community, as many had expected Jack Houlms, who was older and in poor health, to pass first. Yet Jack remained alive and present.

    President Felix Whiskerton, Former President Vector, Former Vice President Alpha, and even Former President Jack Houlms attend Kevin Qubes’ funeral.

    The funeral was a rare moment of unity. All surviving leaders — Felix, Vector, Alpha, and Jack — stood together to honor Qubes. The green flag of Scratch was draped over his casket, symbolizing his role in shaping the platform’s early years.

Last Things Before the Second Term

Whiskerton wanted to finish his first term as if he had not yet been reelected, tying up loose ends before beginning the next chapter.

  • Scratch’s Official Flag

    Felix declared the green flag as the official flag of Scratch. While it had already been used symbolically — placed on caskets of former leaders — Felix made it a formal national emblem of Scratch. From then on, the green flag represented both coding and community spirit.

  • Community Milestones (Late 2017 – Early 2018)

    Several notable events occurred before January 15, 2018:

    • December 2017: The Scratch Team opened the first public Scratch 3.0 preview site, allowing Scratchers to test early builds of the new editor.
    • January 2018: The forums celebrated a major anniversary, with Felix posting a commemorative message about “the voices of Scratchers shaping history.”
    • Early 2018: The “Green Flag Day” tradition began, where Scratchers shared projects honoring the new official flag.

Felix Whiskerton Recommits His Swears and Vows

  • On January 15, 2018, Felix renewed his oath of office and was formally re‑inaugurated for his second term.

    Felix Whiskerton swearing in for the second time

    During his first term, Felix was unable to attend the traditional Presidential Dinner due to the unstable condition of Scratch at the time. By early 2018, however, Scratch was thriving and Scratch 3.0 was on track, so Felix proudly attended the dinner.

    (In the background) President Felix Whiskerton dining with Vice President Abigail Shepard, Forum Governor Plurble J. Shinkton, and head Scratch 3.0 developer Carl Gonzales. (In the foreground, Alpha can be seen, slightly cropped out, looking surprised.)

More Mascot-ing

  • With Scratch 3.0 underway, Whiskerton took on more mascot duties for the Scratch Team. He made frequent appearances at events and represented Scratch in official projects. His account posted a total of 26 projects before Scratch 3.0’s release, with more added afterward.

    Felix also appeared on the homepage during Scratch’s 10th birthday celebration. Because Scratch Day 2017 was close to the anniversary, the event was especially significant, even before the conference where Felix announced Scratch 3.0.

    The Scratch website’s homepage on its 10th birthday!

Abigail Shepard Steps Down

  • On April 6, 2018, Vice President Abigail Shepard shocked the community by announcing her resignation. She explained that her role as Vice President had become increasingly ceremonial, and she wanted to focus on her work as “First Lady of Scratch,” supporting community outreach and education. Shepard emphasized that Scratch needed a Vice President who could dedicate themselves fully to the technical transition to Scratch 3.0.

    This unusual detail may not seem important at first, but it opened the door for a new Vice President — one who would later become the new default sprite.

Larry B. Jefferson Becomes Vice President

  • Larry B. Jefferson, soon nicknamed simply “Scratch Cat,” was the only major feline figure not yet to hold high office. After meeting with President Whiskerton, he was appointed Vice President. Jefferson eventually became the new default sprite, symbolizing Scratch’s fresh start.

    Felix introduced him with the words:

    "We have a new mascot, a new website, and soon we will have a new editor. But we also need a new default sprite — and here he is."

Scratch 3.0 Gets Delayed

  • Context and First Delay Notice (Early 2018)

    As the Scratch Team ramped up work on Scratch 3.0, timelines began to slip. The move from Flash to an HTML5/JavaScript editor proved more complex than expected, and the team chose stability over speed. A “soft delay” was informally acknowledged: Scratch 3.0 would not arrive as early as hoped in 2018.

  • Why the Delay?

    • New technology stack: Rebuilding the editor to run in modern browsers, support touch devices, and perform smoothly required rethinking the engine, paint editor, and extensions system.

    • Backward compatibility: Millions of 2.0 projects had to open and run as expected, which meant careful conversion of blocks, assets, and behaviors.

    • Accessibility and performance: The team prioritized keyboard navigation, screen reader support, and consistent performance on school laptops and tablets.

    • Education-first design: Blocks and UI needed to remain friendly for beginners while empowering advanced creators — a balance that takes time to get right.

  • Roadmap Update (Spring–Summer 2018)

    Instead of a single “big bang” release, the team announced phased milestones: preview sites, educator feedback rounds, and incremental feature drops. The message was clear: better to deliver a robust Scratch 3.0 than rush an unstable editor.

Preview and Prototypes (Through October 2018)

  • Early Previews

    Internal demos and limited previews showcased the new block renderer, updated sprites panel, and revamped paint editor. Feedback from these previews shaped decisions about block sizing, contrast, and layout.

  • Extensions Take Shape

    The new extensions system matured: hardware (micro:bit, Makey Makey), music, pen, and text-to-speech were iterated on. The delay allowed smoother APIs and clearer extension onboarding paths.

  • Paint Editor Revamp

    Vector tools became more precise, bitmap tools more forgiving. Layers, group/ungroup, and transform improvements were tuned for kid-friendly art workflows.

  • Audio and Sound Handling

    Recording, trimming, and playback controls were redesigned with accessibility and classroom use in mind, reducing friction for projects that rely on narration or music.

Accent Color Changes

  • Scratch’s Accent Color Evolves

    Scratch’s visual identity shifted several times in preparation for Scratch 3.0. The homepage accent color moved from the original lighter blue to a deeper, more modern shade. These changes didn’t happen all at once — the header and navigation bar were gradually updated over time.

    Alongside the color changes, the header navigation was streamlined. The “Discuss” button was removed, and the “Help” page was replaced with “Tips”. Later, “Tips” itself was renamed “Ideas”, reflecting Scratch’s emphasis on creativity and exploration. By late 2018, Scratch’s site design was largely ready for the 3.0 launch, with only the editor, project page, and studio pages requiring major overhauls. Many other sections — like Explore and About — were already in the new style.

    At the bottom of the Tips page, right above the footer, there was a section for starter projects. On the side, a sketch of the Scratch 2.0 editor was shown with the new 3.0 art style. Notice the yellow hat block — in Scratch 2.0, hat blocks were a dark brownish orange, which many found unattractive. Scratch 3.0 switched to brighter, more consistent colors, making blocks easier to distinguish and more visually appealing.

The Final Days of Scratch 2.0

  • Countdown to Change

    In the closing weeks of Scratch 2.0, the official Scratch Cat account began posting projects to prepare the community for the big move. These included tutorials on how to save items from the backpack (since it would be reset for 3.0), as well as countdown projects marking the weeks until the new editor’s arrival. Each post built anticipation and reminded Scratchers that a new era was just around the corner.

  • Scratch 2.0 Memories Studio

    A new studio called “Scratch 2.0 Memories” quickly became popular. Scratchers shared projects reflecting on their favorite experiences, from their first remixes to the iconic brownish‑orange hat blocks. The studio became a digital scrapbook, capturing the nostalgia of a generation of Scratchers before the leap into 3.0.

  • Community Spirit

    The forums buzzed with farewell threads, Scratchers posting “goodbye 2.0” animations, and even parody projects imagining Scratch 2.0 as a retiring president. Some users worried about compatibility, while others celebrated the chance to start fresh. The atmosphere was bittersweet: excitement for the future mixed with affection for the past.

  • Technical Preparations

    Behind the scenes, the Scratch Team worked to ensure a smooth transition. Servers were readied for the new editor, migration scripts were tested, and educators were briefed on what to expect. The delay into late 2018 gave developers time to polish features like the paint editor, sound tools, and extensions system.

  • Symbolic Farewell

    On the homepage, Scratch Cat appeared with banners reading “Goodbye Scratch 2.0 — Hello Scratch 3.0!”. The green flag was displayed prominently, symbolizing continuity between eras. Scratchers knew that when they clicked the flag in January 2019, they would be stepping into a brand‑new world.

New Year, New Scratch! (January 2, 2019)

  • The Last Day of Scratch 2.0

    On the morning of January 2, 2019, Scratchers logged in to see Scratch 2.0 for the very last time. The Scratch Cat account posted farewell projects, reminding users to save their backpacks and prepare for the transition. Excitement mixed with nostalgia as the community braced for the change.

    Earlier on 1/2/2019 — the final hours of Scratch 2.0

  • The Transition

    As servers went offline to prepare for the upgrade, Scratch displayed a temporary message announcing the move. Scratchers around the world waited anxiously, refreshing the page to see when the new version would appear.

    Scratch when the server was down, preparing for 3.0

  • The Big Reveal

    Finally, the homepage lit up with the announcement: “Scratch 3.0 is here!”. The new design was modern, colorful, and mobile‑friendly. Scratch 3.0 was a complete reimplementation in HTML5 and JavaScript, freeing Scratch from Flash Player and opening the door to tablets and phones.

    Homepage when Scratch 3.0 was released

  • Glitches and Adjustments

    Not everything went smoothly. Some users reported odd glitches, including strange homepage captures on the Wayback Machine. Others noticed bugs in the editor, such as projects stuck on loading screens. The Scratch Team quickly rolled out fixes in the following days, stabilizing the new platform.

    A strange glitch capture during the transition

  • Settling In

    Within a few days, the homepage normalized, showcasing Scratch’s new look. The “Ideas” page replaced “Tips,” offering tutorials and starter projects. Scratchers began exploring the redesigned editor, testing new blocks, and remixing projects in the fresh environment.

    Scratch’s homepage after a few days

  • The New Editor

    The Scratch 3.0 editor introduced a larger, touch‑friendly block palette, a revamped paint editor, and a redesigned sound editor. Extensions became a major feature, allowing Scratch to connect with devices like micro:bit and LEGO Mindstorms EV3. Hat blocks changed from brownish orange to bright yellow, improving readability and visual appeal. The new file format (.sb3) marked the beginning of a new era.

    Scratch 3.0’s project editor — modern, colorful, and mobile‑ready

Community Reactions

  • Mixed Reception

    Some Scratchers celebrated the new design and mobile compatibility, praising the modern look. Others criticized the interface changes or worried about broken projects. A meme called “Thanos Cat” spread, joking that Scratch 3.0 had “snapped away half the community”. Despite the backlash, Scratch activity remained strong.

  • Positive Momentum

    The Scratch Cat’s project “Scratch 3.0 is here!” received thousands of loves, favorites, and remixes, showing that excitement outweighed concerns. Educators welcomed the move away from Flash, and younger Scratchers embraced the brighter, friendlier interface.

After the Release: Early 2019

  • Felix’s Leadership in the Transition

    With Scratch 3.0 finally live on January 2, 2019, Felix Whiskerton focused on stabilizing the platform. He reassured Scratchers that their projects would be safe, and encouraged them to explore the new features. His calm leadership helped ease tensions among users who were nervous about the changes.

    Felix also launched a campaign called “Explore the New Scratch”, highlighting the new paint editor, sound editor, and extensions system. He appeared in homepage banners and official projects, symbolizing continuity between Scratch 2.0 and 3.0.

  • Early Updates (January–April 2019)

    The Scratch Team quickly rolled out fixes and improvements:

    • January 9, 2019: Bug fixes for projects stuck on loading screens, and love/favorite counts not showing correctly.
    • January 2019: Homepage banner updated from “The new Scratch is here!” to “Create stories, games, and animations”, signaling a new creative era.
    • April 19, 2019: New extension support added — including the Go Direct Force & Acceleration extension — plus more bug fixes.

    Felix praised the Scratch Team’s quick response, calling it proof that “Scratch 3.0 will grow stronger with every update.”

  • Community Reactions

    Reception was mixed. Some Scratchers loved the modern look and mobile support, while others disliked the new interface or worried about broken projects. A meme called “Thanos Cat” spread, joking that Scratch 3.0 had “snapped away half the community.” Felix responded with humor, saying: “Scratch is for everyone — no snap can change that.”

    Despite criticism, Scratch activity remained strong. The Scratch Cat’s project “Scratch 3.0 is here!” received thousands of loves, favorites, and remixes, proving excitement outweighed concerns.

  • Summer 2019 Improvements

    By mid‑2019, Scratch 3.0 was steadily improving:

    • August 1, 2019: Major sound editor update — new tools for selecting portions of audio, copying and pasting, and keyboard shortcuts.
    • Extensions: Text‑to‑Speech, Translate, micro:bit, and LEGO Mindstorms EV3 became popular among Scratchers, expanding what projects could do.
    • Education: Felix promoted Scratch 3.0 in classrooms, encouraging teachers to use the new editor for Hour of Code and STEM programs.

    Felix’s second term was remembered as the “Era of Expansion,” where Scratch grew beyond desktops and into tablets, classrooms, and new creative spaces.

What Now? Election 2019 (The Seventh Election)

  • Context

    By late 2019, Scratch 3.0 had stabilized. The community had adjusted to the new editor, and updates were rolling out regularly. Felix Whiskerton had fulfilled his promise to deliver Scratch 3.0, and now, with his second term ending, the question arose: who would lead Scratch into the next decade?

    The election was more than a formality — it was a referendum on the future. Would Scratch continue the path of steady growth and educational outreach, or pivot toward bold new ideas?

  • Candidates

    • Larry B. Jefferson / Scratch Cat (Democrat)

      Scratch Cat

      Vice President Larry Benjamin Jefferson — now universally known as Scratch Cat — had become the face of Scratch 3.0. As the default sprite and mascot, he symbolized accessibility, creativity, and unity. His campaign focused on continuing Felix’s legacy, expanding Scratch’s reach to underserved schools, and launching new initiatives like “Every Kid Can Code” and “Green Flag Day.”

      Scratch Cat was beloved by many, but some critics argued he was too closely tied to the current administration and lacked bold new ideas.

    • Scratch W. Cat (Republican)

      Scratch W. Cat

      Scratch Walker Cat, son of former president Scratch Vector Walker Cat, emerged as a surprise contender. Unlike his father, Walker embraced change and positioned himself as a reformer. His slogan, “Bring Scratchers Back,” aimed to reconnect with users who had left during the 3.0 transition, promising to restore community features, improve forum visibility, and enhance remix culture.

      Walker’s campaign gained momentum quickly, especially among veteran Scratchers nostalgic for the 2.0 era.

  • The Seventh Debate

    Held in November 2019, the debate was one of the most watched events in Scratch history. Scratch Cat emphasized stability and education, while Walker challenged him with sharp critiques and bold proposals.

    At one point, Walker cleverly turned one of Scratch Cat’s vague statements against him, causing Scratch Cat’s ear to droop in embarrassment — a moment that quickly became a meme.

Set the Stage

As 2020 approached, Scratch stood at a crossroads. With Walker’s unexpected victory, the community braced for a new chapter — one that promised reconciliation, innovation, and renewed energy. Felix Whiskerton prepared to step down, proud of the legacy he left behind. Scratch Cat, though defeated, remained a beloved figure and continued to serve as mascot and mentor.

Side Story: Scratch’s Last Stand Series (2021–2025)

Running parallel to the main presidential timeline is a darker, fictional saga known as Scratch’s Last Stand. These stories imagine an alternate universe where clones, corrupted sprites, and a mysterious entity called The Stickman wage war across the Scratch multiverse. The series is told through a sequence of intense book/game projects released over several years.
